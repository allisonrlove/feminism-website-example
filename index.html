Where does feminism begin?

Sometimes with a feeling that something is wrong. A moment that does not make sense. A rule that seems natural until it hurts someone. Feminist thinking can begin by staying with that feeling long enough to ask what made it possible.

Ahmed describes feminism as beginning in sensation: registering an injustice before we necessarily have the language to explain it. (Ahmed, 2017, pp. 21–23)

01 / A few ways to look

Ideas that change
what you can see.

These are not isolated concepts. They overlap, travel between the personal and political, and become useful in the situations where we need them.

01✳

The feminist killjoy

The person who names sexism, racism, or exclusion — and is then blamed for spoiling the mood. The discomfort is treated as the problem, rather than the injustice being pointed out.

Try asking: What has to stay unspoken for everyone to seem happy?

Ahmed, 2017, pp. 37–41; ch. 1

02↗

Orientation & direction

Social worlds point us toward certain futures: particular jobs, relationships, bodies, and forms of happiness. These directions can feel like common sense because they are repeated and supported.

Try asking: Who is this path designed to fit?

Ahmed, 2017, ch. 2, pp. 43–63

03↯

Willfulness

A girl or woman who refuses the place assigned to her may be called difficult, stubborn, or out of line. Ahmed reclaims willfulness as a resource for refusing unjust demands to obey.

Try asking: Whose refusal gets called a problem?

Ahmed, 2017, ch. 3, pp. 65–87

04▤

Institutional walls

Exclusion is not always a dramatic act. It can be built into routines, expectations, workloads, and systems that help some people move forward while slowing others down.

Try asking: What keeps happening — and who benefits?

Ahmed, 2017, chs. 4–6, especially pp. 151–59

05✂

Feminist snap

What looks like a sudden breaking point often has a long history. A snap can make accumulated pressure visible; it can also become collective action when people connect their experiences.

Try asking: What history is hidden inside this moment?

Ahmed, 2017, ch. 8, pp. 187–211

06⌂

Feminist home-making

Feminism is not only a theory to study elsewhere. It is work we do in the places we live, work, learn, and gather — changing relationships and building forms of support.

Try asking: What would make this place more livable for more people?

Ahmed, 2017, Introduction, pp. 7–17

02 / Look closer

Feminism is intersectional.

Power does not operate along one track. Gender is entangled with race, class, sexuality, disability, colonial histories, and other structures.

NO SINGLE
STORY✳

Ahmed draws on bell hooks’s definition of feminism as a movement to end sexism, sexual exploitation, and sexual oppression. She insists that these cannot be understood separately from racism, colonialism, slavery, and the organization of labor.

Intersectionality is therefore not an optional add-on. It is a starting point for understanding how power works — and for asking whose experiences a movement has left out.

Ahmed, 2017, Introduction, pp. 5–6, drawing on hooks, 2000, p. 33.

Why does this matter in practice?

A policy or campaign can challenge sexism while still reproducing racism, ableism, class exclusion, or transphobia. An intersectional approach asks how these systems meet in lived experience, rather than treating them as separate issues.

03 / Put it to work

Small questions.
Structural thinking.

Feminist practice is not about having the perfect answer. It is about learning to notice patterns, make connections, and change what we can — while staying open to correction.

At home

Who does the planning, remembering, cleaning, and caring? Which tasks are treated as natural rather than work?

At work or school

Whose expertise is assumed? Who gets interrupted, mentored, promoted, believed, or asked to do invisible labor?

In public

Whose safety is prioritized? Whose anger is taken seriously? Who is expected to explain or defend their presence?

In a movement

Who is included in the “we”? Who is asked to wait? What forms of difference or disagreement are being made difficult to express?

A killjoy survival kit

Ahmed’s survival kit is not a universal checklist. It is a reminder that feminist work takes energy, and that we need resources and one another to keep going.

05 / Not the end

Make room.
Make trouble.
Make connections.

Living otherwise is collective work. There is no finished version of feminism waiting at the end — only the ongoing work of making our worlds more just and more livable.

Ahmed, 2017, Conclusion 2, “A Killjoy Manifesto,” especially pp. 251–56.

A note on the project / 00

ABOUT
THE PRINT.

An independent introduction to feminist ideas, built from a conversation with one book — and meant to travel beyond it.

What this is

This website introduces themes and arguments developed in Sara Ahmed’s Living a Feminist Life (2017). It is not a substitute for the book, a chapter-by-chapter summary, or a claim that one text can represent every feminist tradition. Instead, it uses Ahmed’s work as a starting point for explaining how feminism can help us understand ordinary life, social structures, and collective change.

How to read it

Move through the page, open the questions, follow the citations, and use the prompts as invitations to think — not as a test. The language here is introductory and condensed. Where a claim is drawn from Ahmed, a chapter or page reference is provided. Other writers are identified in the text and listed in the references.

About the visual language

The design borrows from the energy of DIY zines and risograph printing: layered inks, imperfect registration, paper-like texture, cut-paper shapes, and a limited palette. The visual style is original and does not reproduce the book’s cover or artwork.

A note on scope

Ahmed’s account is explicitly intersectional and draws substantially on Black feminist, feminist-of-color, queer, and anticolonial scholarship. This short site cannot do justice to the breadth of those traditions. The references are offered as paths outward, not as a closed canon. Read the cited writers in their own words wherever possible.
